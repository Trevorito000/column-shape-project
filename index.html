The Effect of the Shape of a Cardstock Column on Its Load-Bearing Capacity

Science fair project. Three column shapes, nine tests, one question: which shape holds the most weight?

Rate this poster

Problem statement

What is the effect of the shape of a cardstock column (triangular, square, or cylindrical) on its load-bearing capacity, measured as the maximum weight (g) supported before collapse?

Hypothesis

It is hypothesized that if the shape of a cardstock column is triangular, then it will support a greater weight (g) before collapse than square (control) or cylindrical columns of identical size, material, and mass, because a triangle is a geometrically rigid shape that distributes the applied load evenly and leaves no weak points.

Background

A triangle is the only polygon that cannot change shape unless the length of at least one of its sides changes; this property is called geometric rigidity (ScienceInsights, 2026). A square, in contrast, can deform into a parallelogram while its side lengths remain the same. When a load is applied to a corner of a triangle, it is transmitted along its sides as compression and tension (Let's Talk Science, n.d.), which is why the triangle is the simplest rigid form of a truss (University of Sydney, n.d.; Ungvarsky, 2019). Engineers apply this principle through triangulation in structures such as cranes, truss bridges, and electricity pylons. Hollow columns, like those tested here, are valued for their resistance to buckling, and their cross-sectional shape strongly influences their mechanical behavior (Shima et al., 2020).

Materials

  1. 9 sheets of cardstock, identical in type, thickness, and dimensions, each weighed to confirm equal mass; the resulting columns were approximately 24 cm tall
  2. Metric ruler
  3. Pencil
  4. Scissors
  5. Adhesive tape, pre-cut into 9 identical pieces of 5 cm
  6. Digital scale (g)
  7. Books, used as weights, in sufficient quantity
  8. Flat, firm base on which to place the columns
  9. Camera or mobile phone, to video-record each test
  10. Data table or notebook, to record results

Procedures

Preparation

  1. Cut 9 sheets of cardstock to identical dimensions and weigh each one on the digital scale. Trim any sheet that differs until all have the same mass.
  2. Using a pencil and ruler, mark the fold lines on each sheet according to its shape (triangular, square, or cylindrical).
  3. Build and test one practice column of each shape, and adjust the technique if needed.

Construction

  1. Fold each sheet along its marked lines, pressing firmly on every fold.
  2. Seal each column with one 5 cm piece of tape, placed in the same position on every column.
  3. Let the columns rest for several minutes. Build 3 columns of each shape, for a total of 9 columns.

Strength test

  1. Define the collapse point used for all tests: the moment the column collapses and can no longer support the weight.
  2. Stand the first column upright on the flat, firm base, verifying that it is straight and stable.
  3. Begin video recording. Place books on top of the column, one at a time, until the column collapses.
  4. Record the total weight (g) supported at collapse.
  5. Repeat steps 8-10 for all 9 columns, alternating the order of the shapes.

Data analysis

  1. Record the results in the data table and calculate the mean and range of the weight supported for each shape.

Results

The results of the experiment were as follows. Triangular columns supported the most weight, with a mean of 3513 g (range: 544 g); square (control) columns averaged 2940 g (range: 317 g) and cylindrical columns 2855 g (range: 455 g). Triangular columns supported 19.5% more weight than the control, and every triangular trial exceeded every square and cylindrical trial.

Conclusion

This experiment investigated the effect of column shape (triangular, square, or cylindrical) on the weight a cardstock column could support before collapsing. The hypothesis, that triangular columns would support more weight than square and cylindrical columns, was supported by the data. Triangular columns reached a mean of 3513 g, compared with 2940 g for the square (control) and 2855 g for the cylindrical columns, and all three triangular trials exceeded every trial of the other two shapes.

The major finding was that the triangular shape supported approximately 19.5% more weight than the control and 23.0% more than the cylindrical shape, while the square and cylindrical shapes performed similarly (3.0% difference). This result is consistent with the principle of geometric rigidity; however, because the cross-sectional shape of hollow columns strongly influences their mechanical behavior (Shima et al., 2020), other factors may also have contributed. Possible sources of error include the small number of trials (n = 3 per shape); the high value of triangular column 3 (3865 g), which was 512 g above column 2 and widened the triangular range; small variations in folding, tape placement, or seam alignment among columns; and the use of books as weights, whose relatively large increments limited the precision of the exact collapse load.

For future studies, I recommend increasing the number of trials per shape, adding other shapes (e.g., pentagonal or hexagonal), varying column height and wall thickness, and using a calibrated loading system that applies the weight in smaller, uniform increments. In the real world, these findings relate to the use of triangulation in cranes, truss bridges, electricity pylons, and other load-bearing structures. These columns are small-scale models, so real structures would also depend on material, scale, and design.
